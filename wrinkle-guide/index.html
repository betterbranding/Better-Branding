There are two kinds of wrinkles

This is the single most useful thing to know before choosing. These treatments are not really competitors. They fix different problems, which is why many women in their 40s end up using a toxin and a collagen treatment together.

Type 1

Expression lines

Frown "11s," forehead lines, crow's feet. They appear (or deepen) when you move your face. Caused by repeated muscle contraction.

Best fix: Botox or Xeomin (relax the muscle)
Type 2

Etched lines & crepiness

Fine lines that stay even when your face is still, thinning skin, rough texture, under-eye crepiness. Caused mostly by collagen loss and sun.

Possible fix: PRP/PRF (collagen support), plus retinoids, lasers, or filler
30%
According to the American Academy of Dermatology, women's skin loses about 30% of its collagen during the first five years of menopause. That's why many women notice "etched" lines and texture changes picking up in their mid-to-late 40s, even with a great skincare routine.

Everything about each option

How it works

A purified protein that temporarily blocks the nerve signal telling specific muscles to contract. The skin over those muscles stops folding, so lines soften and new ones are less likely to etch in. FDA-approved for cosmetic use since 2002 for frown lines, now also for forehead lines, crow's feet, and neck (platysma) bands.

Benefits

  • Most-studied cosmetic injectable in the world, with 20+ years of data.
  • Quick: about 10–15 minutes, little to no downtime.
  • Predictable, visible results on expression lines.
  • Regular use can help prevent lines from deepening over time. A long-term study of repeated upper-face treatments found effectiveness and safety stayed consistently high.
  • Approved for the most face and neck areas of any toxin.

Treatments & maintenance

Typically 3–4 visits per year. Labels say no more often than every 3 months. Some long-term users find they can stretch visits a bit further over time.

Health risks

CommonPinpoint bruising, tenderness, mild headache for a day or two.
UncommonDroopy eyelid (about 3% in frown-line trials), heavy or uneven brows. Temporary, usually weeks.
RareAllergic reaction. FDA boxed warning for toxin spreading beyond the injection site (trouble swallowing, breathing, vision). Not confirmed at cosmetic doses, but seek care right away if it happens.

Who should not get it (or should check first)

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding (not enough safety data; most providers wait).
  • Allergy to any botulinum toxin or its ingredients (contains human albumin).
  • Infection or active breakout at the injection site.
  • Neuromuscular conditions: myasthenia gravis, Lambert-Eaton, ALS.
  • Trouble swallowing or breathing, or prior eyelid surgery or weak eyelids.

Cost

$10–$16 per unit in DFW ($10–$20 nationally). Frown lines alone: about 20 units ($200–$320). Full upper face (frown + forehead + crow's feet): about 40–64 units, roughly $400–$1,000 per visit.

How it works

The same active ingredient type as Botox (botulinum toxin type A), made by Merz. The difference: Xeomin is purified to remove the "complexing proteins" that surround the toxin in Botox. That's why it's nicknamed the "naked" toxin. It's dosed roughly 1:1 with Botox. FDA-approved for frown lines (2011) and, since July 2024, for treating forehead lines, frown lines, and crow's feet together.

Benefits

  • Matched Botox for results and duration in head-to-head studies (month 1: 95.7% vs 99.2%; month 4: 62.1% vs 67.2%).
  • No complexing proteins, which may lower the theoretical risk of developing antibodies that make toxins stop working. This is debated, and resistance is rare with any brand at cosmetic doses.
  • Often priced $1–$3 less per unit than Botox.
  • Doesn't need refrigeration before mixing, which makes storage simpler for the clinic.
  • A good option to try if Botox seems to be wearing off faster than it used to.

Same rhythm as Botox: 3–4 visits a year, no more often than every 12 weeks per the label. Some people notice it kicks in a day or two slower, but studies show comparable onset.

Health risks

CommonInjection-site bruising (about 2% in upper-face trials), headache, tenderness.
UncommonDroopy eyelid or brow, asymmetry, dry eyes. Temporary.
RareAllergic reaction. Same FDA boxed warning as all toxins for distant spread of effect.

Who should not get it (or should check first)

  • Pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • Allergy to any botulinum toxin or its ingredients (contains human albumin and sucrose).
  • Infection at the injection site.
  • Neuromuscular conditions such as myasthenia gravis or Lambert-Eaton syndrome.
  • Swallowing or breathing problems.

Cost

$8–$18 per unit nationally, commonly $10–$13. Frown lines: about 20 units ($200–$300). Full upper face: roughly $400–$850 per visit.

How it works

A small tube of your blood is drawn and spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets, which carry growth factors. The concentrate is injected into the skin or applied during microneedling (the "vampire facial") to encourage collagen and improve texture.

PRP vs PRF: PRF (platelet-rich fibrin) is a newer version spun more slowly without anticoagulant. It forms a soft gel that may release growth factors over a longer period. Reviews so far show neither one is clearly better. For this guide, "PFP" is treated as PRF; ask your provider exactly what their version is.

The honest picture: In a U.S. randomized trial where participants averaged age 46 (mostly women), blinded dermatologists saw no measurable difference in fine lines after one PRP session. But participants themselves rated their skin's texture and wrinkles as improved at 6 months. Studies are small and use different methods, so results are less predictable than with toxins.

Benefits

  • Uses your own blood, so no foreign substance and essentially no allergy risk.
  • Targets texture, fine "crepey" lines, glow, and under-eye skin, which toxins don't address.
  • Pairs well with a toxin: muscles relaxed by Botox/Xeomin plus skin quality from PRP.
  • Results build gradually and look natural.

Improvement builds over 2–3 months after the series. A review of under-eye studies found PRF gains often faded by about 6 months, which is why maintenance matters.

Health risks

CommonRedness, swelling, and bruising for 1–3 days (with microneedling, a sunburn-like look). Soreness at the blood draw.
UncommonLightheadedness during the draw, small bumps, temporary dark spots (more likely on deeper skin tones after microneedling).
RareInfection, or risk from improper blood handling, which the AAD calls the biggest risk. Choose a medical practice with sterile, single-use kits.

Who should not get it (or should check first)

  • Blood or platelet disorders, low platelet count, or anemia.
  • On blood thinners for heart disease (AAD says not recommended).
  • Any blood cancer, active cancer or chemo, or skin cancer in the treated area.
  • Hepatitis C, HIV, active infection, fever, or an active cold sore or acne flare in the area.
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding (most providers wait). History of keloid scarring, if microneedling.

Cost

$500–$1,500 per session (AAD range: $250–$1,500). PRF and face + neck sessions trend higher. A series of 3 is typically $1,500–$3,600, often with a package discount. Not FDA-approved specifically for wrinkles (the devices are cleared, the facial use is off-label), and insurance doesn't cover it.

Specific to this decade

Things that matter in your 40s

Perimenopause changes the game

Collagen loss speeds up as estrogen declines, so lines shift from mostly "expression" to a mix with "etched" lines and thinner skin. That's when a toxin alone may feel less complete, and adding a collagen treatment (PRP/PRF, prescription retinoid, laser, or microneedling) makes sense.

Natural-looking dosing

Women in their 40s often prefer softening over freezing. Ask for a conservative first round with a 2-week check. It's easy to add a few units; you can't take them away. Over-treating the forehead in particular can make brows feel heavy.

Pregnancy planning

If pregnancy is possible, mention it. Toxins and PRP are generally deferred during pregnancy and breastfeeding because there isn't enough safety data.

Weight-loss medications (GLP-1s)

There's no known direct interaction between semaglutide or tirzepatide and these treatments. But faster weight loss can reduce facial volume, which toxins don't fix and PRP only modestly helps. That's usually a filler or collagen-stimulator conversation.

The foundation still matters most

Daily SPF 30+ and a retinoid (over-the-counter retinol or prescription tretinoin) are the best-proven long-term wrinkle prevention. Injectables work better and last longer on skin that's protected.

A common combo plan

Toxin for frown, forehead, and crow's feet 3–4 times a year, plus a PRP/PRF series in a slower season, then 1–2 maintenance sessions a year. Space them out: many providers do toxin and microneedling on different days, or microneedle first.
